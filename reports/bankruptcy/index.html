Bankruptcy Appraisal

A defensible value for your home as of the petition date, written for Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 cases.

A bankruptcy petition asks a simple question with serious consequences: what is the house worth? The number you list on Schedule A/B shapes how the homestead exemption applies, whether a trustee sees equity worth pursuing, and how a Chapter 13 plan is measured. Brian Ward has appraised homes across San Diego County and Riverside County since 2004, and he personally prepares every report. He works for filers, debtor attorneys and trustees, and the report reads the same no matter who orders it: an independent opinion of market value, supported by nearby sales and clear photographs. You get a written quote before anything starts, and the fee is due when the report is delivered.

How it fits a Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 case

In a Chapter 7 case, the trustee's job is to find property that can be sold for the benefit of unsecured creditors. For a house, the trustee looks at market value, subtracts the liens, the homestead exemption, and the realistic costs of a sale, then decides whether anything meaningful is left. A careful appraisal answers that math with evidence instead of an online estimate. Many filers find the trustee accepts a well-supported report and moves on; others use it to negotiate if a question comes up after the 341 meeting of creditors.

Chapter 13 uses value differently. Because unsecured creditors must get no less under the plan than a Chapter 7 liquidation would have paid them, the appraisal supports the best-interest-of-creditors test. Value is also key when a HELOC or second mortgage may have no collateral behind it: if the home's value falls below what is owed on the first loan, the debtor's attorney can bring a valuation motion to have the junior lien treated as unsecured. On a rental or other property that is not the debtor's residence, a cramdown argument can turn on the appraised figure as well.

In both chapters the effective date usually matters more than the inspection date. Courts look at value as of the petition date, so a report prepared months later often carries a retrospective effective date and relies on sales that closed around the filing. Brian can also prepare a report before filing, which lets your attorney plan with a real number in hand. If a creditor objects to your valuation, the same report becomes the starting point for any response, and testimony can be arranged separately. Brian is an appraiser, not a lawyer, so questions about exemptions and strategy belong with your bankruptcy attorney.

Chapter 7: what the trustee is weighing

A Chapter 7 trustee is not trying to take every house. The trustee is looking for net value: market price, minus the mortgage balances, minus the exemption the debtor claims, minus a commission and closing costs. When that remainder is small or negative, a sale makes no sense for creditors. The appraisal is the piece of that calculation most likely to be questioned, because the other numbers come from loan statements and the exemption statute. Brian's reports show the comparable sales he relied on, the adjustments he made for size, condition and location, and photographs that document what a buyer would actually see. Deferred maintenance, an outdated kitchen or a failing roof are recorded plainly, since they affect what the home would bring. The result is a report a trustee can read in minutes and test against the evidence.

Chapter 13: plans, liquidation analysis and lien stripping

In a five-year or three-year plan, the appraisal does several jobs at once. It supports the liquidation analysis that shows unsecured creditors are receiving at least what a Chapter 7 would have paid them. It can establish that a junior mortgage or HELOC has no equity behind it, which is the factual basis for a motion to value and strip that lien. If the debtor owns a rental or vacation property, value can also bear on how a secured claim is treated. Because each of these uses depends on the petition date, Brian identifies the effective date at the start and picks comparable sales that closed near it. Attorneys receive a report formatted for filing as an exhibit, with the effective date, scope of work and appraiser certification stated on the first pages.

The petition date and retrospective values

Many bankruptcy appraisals are ordered after the case is already open. That is normal. The appraisal simply states an effective date that matches the petition, and the analysis uses sales from that period rather than today's market. Brian notes any changes to the home since filing, such as a new roof or storm damage, and explains whether he valued the property as it stood on the petition date. When the house was inspected later, the report says so and describes how the earlier condition was established, often through the owner's description, listing photos, or permits. Older retrospective dates take more research, and the quote reflects that. If you are still deciding whether to file, a current-value report gives your attorney a baseline that can be updated if the filing date moves.

Condos, rentals and 2-4 unit properties

Not every debtor owns a detached house. HOA condos carry their own questions: special assessments, pending litigation against the association, and rental caps can all affect what a buyer will pay. Brian reviews what is available about the project and reports anything that matters to value. Rentals and small multi-unit buildings are valued using sales of similar income properties, and for 2-4 unit buildings the report can include rent comparables and an income approach when that helps explain value. These details matter in bankruptcy because a non-residence property usually has no homestead protection, so the trustee looks at it closely. A report that addresses rents, condition and unit mix gives the trustee and the attorney the same facts to work from, which tends to keep the discussion short.

Community property and the non-filing spouse

California is a community property state, and that shapes bankruptcy cases in ways that surprise people. When one spouse files, community property generally comes into the estate even if the other spouse never signs the petition. The house is valued as a whole, and the attorney then works through how title, separate contributions and exemptions apply. Brian's role is narrow and useful: he provides the market value of the entire property as of the right date, with no allocation between spouses unless counsel asks for a specific scope. If the couple is also separating, the same home may need a value for the family court case at a different date. Brian can prepare both effective dates in coordinated reports so the numbers do not contradict each other without explanation.

Bankruptcy Appraisal questions

Do I need an appraisal to file bankruptcy?

The law does not require one, but you must list the value of your home under penalty of perjury. An online estimate or a guess can be challenged by the trustee. Many attorneys ask for an appraisal whenever there is meaningful equity, a junior mortgage that might be stripped, or a property type that automated estimates handle poorly. Your bankruptcy attorney can tell you whether your case calls for one.

What date will the appraisal use?

Usually the petition date, because that is when the bankruptcy estate is created. If you have not filed yet, the report can use a current date and be updated if needed. If you filed months ago, the report uses a retrospective effective date and relies on sales from around the filing. Your attorney should confirm the date before the order is placed.

Can the trustee use their own appraiser instead?

Yes. A trustee may obtain a broker opinion or a separate appraisal, especially when the numbers are close. A well-documented report gives your attorney something concrete to compare against. Differences usually come down to the choice of comparable sales and the condition of the house, which is why Brian records condition carefully and explains every adjustment he makes.

Will a lower value help my case?

The goal is an accurate value, not a low one. An appraisal that ignores good sales or exaggerates problems tends to fall apart once a trustee looks at it, and it can create credibility issues for the filer. Brian reports what the evidence supports. How that number fits the exemption and the rest of your case is a question for your bankruptcy attorney.
