Estate & Trust Appraisal

Clear values for trustees and executors who must inventory, divide, sell or distribute real estate.

Successor trustees and executors carry real responsibility. They must account for every asset, treat beneficiaries fairly, and often decide whether to sell, rent or distribute a home. Brian Ward prepares estate and trust appraisals that give these fiduciaries a documented starting point. Reports can value a home as of the date of death, as of a later distribution date, or as of today when a sale or buyout is being considered. Brian works directly with family members, attorneys and CPAs, and he writes every report personally. Fees are quoted in writing before work begins, and law firms or trusts can be invoiced when that is easier for the accounting.

Where this meets a bankruptcy case

Trust and estate administration can run into bankruptcy from either direction. A beneficiary who is in an open bankruptcy case may have their inheritance claimed by the trustee, especially when the right to inherit arises within 180 days after filing. The bankruptcy trustee will want to know what that share is worth, and a value for the trust's real estate is usually needed. On the other side, the person who created the trust may have died owing significant debt, and the estate itself may be insolvent. Creditors' claims, a possible sale, and the order of payment all depend on what the property will bring. Brian provides the market value; the attorneys decide how it applies. When both a probate or trust case and a bankruptcy case need values, he coordinates the effective dates and explains any differences so that neither court sees conflicting numbers without context.

What fiduciaries need from an appraisal

A trustee or executor has to show that decisions were reasonable. If one sibling takes the house and another receives cash, the value used should hold up if anyone later questions it. If the trust sells to an outsider, a recent appraisal helps show the price was fair. Brian's reports are written with that scrutiny in mind. They describe the property, its condition and its neighborhood, show the comparable sales used and why, and state the effective date and intended users clearly. Fiduciaries frequently share the report with beneficiaries, which can lower tension simply because everyone sees the same evidence at once instead of hearing secondhand numbers. A clear report also makes the trustee's final accounting easier to prepare and explain later.

Distribution, buyouts and sales

Many trusts hold a family home that one beneficiary wants to keep. A current-value appraisal gives the family a fair number for the buyout and the trustee a record supporting the decision. If the home will be sold, the report helps set a sensible list price and judge offers. Brian can also flag condition items that tend to come up during a buyer's inspection, which allows the trustee to decide early whether to repair or price around them. When there is more than one property, such as a residence plus a rental, he can appraise each and deliver the reports together so the accounting stays organized and the effective dates match.

Working with attorneys and CPAs

Estate attorneys and tax preparers often need slightly different things from the same appraisal. The attorney may want a date-of-death value for the inventory and a current value for distribution. The CPA may care mainly about the stepped-up basis. Brian asks up front who will read the report and what decisions it supports, then prepares one or more reports that fit. He keeps communication simple: one point of contact, a written quote, and a delivery date. If questions arise after delivery, he answers them directly. When an estate goes to litigation, he can provide testimony about his work, billed separately and quoted ahead of time. You receive the report as a PDF, ready to send to each advisor involved.

What the report includes

  • Market value opinion at the date of death, at a distribution date, or at the present
  • Comparable sales grid with explanations of each adjustment
  • Description of condition and photographs (inspected reports)
  • Identification of the trust or estate as client and listed intended users
  • Location map, scope of work and signed certification
  • Coordinated reports for multiple properties or dates when needed

Estate & Trust Appraisal questions

Does a trust need a date-of-death or a current appraisal?

Often both. The date-of-death value usually sets the tax basis and the inventory figure, while a current value helps with sales and distributions. Your estate attorney or CPA can confirm which dates are needed. Brian can prepare both at once, which typically costs less than ordering them separately months apart.

Can the trust pay the appraisal fee?

Yes. Many successor trustees pay from the trust account, and law firms can be invoiced directly. The fee is due when the report is delivered, by card, check or bank transfer. Brian provides a written quote first so the trustee can document the expense. Trust accountants appreciate having that quote on file alongside the final invoice and the delivered report.

What if beneficiaries disagree with the value?

Disagreements happen. The report lays out the sales and reasoning so anyone can review them. If a beneficiary has information about a sale or a property feature that was missed, Brian will consider it and explain whether it changes the analysis. Disputes that continue may need legal guidance from the trust's attorney.

Do you appraise properties outside the two counties?

Inspected reports focus on San Diego County and Riverside County. Desktop reports can sometimes cover California properties farther away when good sales data exists. Ask when you call and Brian will tell you honestly whether he can do the work well. If he is not the right fit for a distant property, he will say so rather than take the assignment anyway.
