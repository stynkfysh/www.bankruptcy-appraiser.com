Appraisal report

Pre-Listing Appraisal

An independent value before you list, so the price starts from evidence rather than optimism.

Sellers often hear two very different numbers: what an agent hopes to list at and what a buyer's lender will eventually support. Getting an appraisal before you list gives you an independent number before the home hits the market. Brian Ward walks the home, notes features and flaws a buyer's appraiser will see, and compares it to recent sales in the neighborhood. He has no commission riding on the result. That independence helps you set a price that attracts buyers without leaving money behind, and it can reduce surprises when the buyer's appraisal arrives. The report also helps you decide which repairs are worth doing and which are better handled through price.

Where this meets a bankruptcy case

Selling a house is sometimes part of the bankruptcy plan. In Chapter 13, a debtor may propose to sell the home within the plan period to pay creditors or a mortgage arrearage, and the court and trustee will want to know the sale price is reasonable. In Chapter 7, a trustee who decides to sell a property with non-exempt equity needs a sensible list price and evidence for the court when approving the sale. A pre-listing appraisal answers both needs. It shows the expected market value, documents the condition that may affect offers, and gives the attorney or trustee support if a creditor questions the price. Debtors negotiating with a trustee sometimes use the appraisal to propose buying back the estate's interest instead of selling. Brian provides the value; decisions about selling, timing and court approval belong with the trustee, the debtor and their attorneys.

Pricing with an independent opinion

Agents are skilled at marketing, but their pricing advice can be shaped by the desire to win the listing. A seller who overprices may sit on the market for weeks and then cut the price anyway, which can make buyers wary. Brian's pre-listing appraisal offers a second, independent view grounded in closed sales rather than active listings. He explains which sales are most comparable and why, so you can discuss pricing with your agent from a stronger position. Many agents welcome the report because it gives them evidence to share with buyers who question the price. The report also lets you explain the price to a hesitant co-owner, heir or trustee with confidence. That shared understanding can make later decisions about offers smoother.

Spotting appraisal problems early

A buyer's lender will order an appraisal, and if it comes in below the contract price, the deal may fall apart or require renegotiation. A pre-listing appraisal lets you see likely issues in advance: unpermitted additions, a garage conversion, a roof near the end of its life, or a lack of nearby sales for a unique home. Knowing about these items before listing gives you time to gather permits, make repairs, or adjust expectations. Brian notes the items that tend to concern appraisers and buyers alike, so you can make informed choices instead of reacting under deadline pressure. Sellers who address these points before listing tend to face fewer last-minute renegotiations. It also helps to have the documents ready when a buyer's agent asks.

Repairs worth making before you sell

Not every repair pays for itself. Fresh paint and fixing obvious defects usually help, while a full kitchen remodel right before selling often does not return its cost. Brian's walkthrough can identify the items that a buyer's appraiser is most likely to adjust for, which helps you focus limited money where it matters. He does not act as a contractor or recommend specific vendors, but his notes on condition give you a practical starting list. For estates, trustees and Chapter 13 debtors working within tight budgets, that guidance can prevent spending on improvements that will not change the sale price. Small fixes such as sticking doors or missing trim often matter more than people assume, because they shape a buyer's first impression.

What the report includes

  • Opinion of current market value based on closed comparable sales
  • Notes on features and condition items likely to affect buyer and lender appraisals
  • Interior and exterior photographs (inspected reports)
  • Comparable sales grid with plain-language adjustments
  • Location map, scope of work and signed certification
  • Report suitable for a trustee's sale motion or a Chapter 13 plan sale

Pre-Listing Appraisal questions

Why pay for an appraisal when my agent gives a free price opinion?

An agent's opinion is useful, but it often leans on active listings and can be influenced by the goal of winning your listing. An appraisal is an independent analysis based on closed sales, prepared by someone with no stake in the sale. Many sellers use both to compare. Comparing the two can reveal where expectations differ.

Will the buyer's lender use your appraisal?

No. Lenders order their own appraisals through approved channels. Brian's report helps you anticipate what that appraiser may find and set a price that has a better chance of being supported. Sharing your report with your agent can help them prepare for the lender's appraisal. Some agents attach it to the listing file as pricing support.

Can a Chapter 13 debtor use this to sell the house in the plan?

It can help. Selling property during a Chapter 13 case often requires court approval, and a supportable value makes the proposed price easier to justify. Your bankruptcy attorney will handle the motion and timing. Brian provides the market value evidence. The report should carry a recent effective date so the court and trustee can see the price reflects current conditions.

For how long does a pre-listing appraisal stay useful?

Markets move, so the report reflects conditions on its effective date. If your listing is delayed several months, or if prices shift noticeably, an update may be worthwhile. Brian can provide an updated value at a reduced fee when the property has not changed. The update keeps the same format so it is easy to compare.
