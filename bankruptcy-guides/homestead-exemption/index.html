The California Homestead Exemption and Why Value Drives It

The exemption protects a slice of your home equity, and you cannot know whether it covers you until you know what the home is worth.

Key points

  • California filers choose between the 704 and 703 exemption systems; they cannot be mixed.
  • The 704 homestead is the greater of $300,000 or the county median sale price, capped at $600,000, adjusted annually for inflation.
  • Your attorney confirms the exact protected amount; the appraisal supplies the market value side.
  • Estimates that are too high or too low can each cause problems with the trustee.
  • Costs of sale and condition affect what a trustee could actually net.
  • This page is general information from an appraiser, not legal advice. Brian Ward is not a lawyer, and nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship; ask a bankruptcy attorney how the law applies to your case.

What an exemption does

When you file bankruptcy, the law lets you keep certain property out of reach of creditors. These protections are called exemptions. California requires filers to use the state's exemption scheme rather than the federal list, and the state offers two separate systems. For a homeowner, the biggest question is how much home equity is protected. Equity is roughly the market value of the home minus what you owe against it. If your protected amount is larger than your equity, the trustee in a Chapter 7 case generally has nothing to gain by selling. If equity exceeds the exemption, the excess is exposed. In Chapter 13, that exposed equity typically shapes how much the plan has to pay. Either way, market value is the starting point, and an appraisal is how that number is established.

The 704 system in plain English

Under the system found in California Code of Civil Procedure section 704, the homestead exemption is the greater of $300,000 or the county median sale price for a single-family home, capped at $600,000, with annual inflation adjustments. Because the median in many parts of San Diego and Riverside Counties varies over time, the exact protected figure for a particular filing is something your attorney confirms for your county and filing date. The appraiser does not decide your exemption amount. The appraisal supplies the other half of that comparison, a well-supported estimate of your home's likely sale price on the date that matters. Your attorney then lines the two up against your loan balances and costs of sale to see where you stand.

The 703 system and when it comes up

California's other set of exemptions, found in section 703.140, works differently. It has a much smaller homestead amount but adds a flexible wildcard that can be applied to other property. Filers with little or no home equity, renters, or people with significant cash or other assets sometimes do better under this system. You must choose one system or the other; you cannot pick the best parts of each. For married filers, both spouses generally have to agree on the choice. Which system fits is a legal decision based on your full set of assets, and only your bankruptcy attorney should make it. An accurate home value is part of the information your attorney needs to compare the two systems sensibly, which is why the appraisal is often ordered early.

Why a rough estimate can backfire

Homeowners sometimes rely on an online estimate or a neighbor's sale to guess their equity. The risk runs both ways. A number that is too high can make it look as though equity exceeds the exemption, pushing a filer toward a more expensive Chapter 13 plan or a choice of exemptions that is not ideal. A number that is too low may invite the trustee to challenge the schedules and order a separate valuation, adding delay and expense. An appraisal compares your home with actual closed sales near the filing date, adjusts for differences in size, age, lot, and condition, and documents the reasoning. That gives your attorney a value that can be explained to a trustee and, if necessary, defended.

Costs of sale and the trustee's net

Even when equity appears to exceed the exemption on paper, a trustee looks at what would actually be left after a sale. Broker commissions, escrow and title charges, transfer taxes, repairs needed to market the home, and the trustee's own administrative costs all reduce the net. Brian does not calculate legal costs or tell anyone what a trustee will do, but a clear report with condition photos helps everyone estimate a realistic net. A home that needs a new roof and a plumbing repair before it can be listed is a different prospect than one that is move-in ready, and the report should say which it is. Those details often matter as much as the headline value when equity is close to the line.

Timing, ownership changes, and getting advice

Federal bankruptcy law limits the homestead protection in some situations, for example when a home was acquired relatively recently before filing or when equity came from certain transfers. Married couples, co-owners, and homes held in a trust raise their own questions about how much of the property belongs to the bankruptcy estate. These are issues to raise with your attorney before filing. When you are ready for a value, call (760) 557-2147. Brian will ask for the address, the petition or planned filing date, and who owns the property, then give a written quote. The report can address the whole property and, where your attorney asks, note the ownership interest being valued. Ownership details often change the analysis more than people expect.
