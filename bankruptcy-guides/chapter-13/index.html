Chapter 13 and Your Home: How an Appraisal Supports the Plan

A Chapter 13 plan lets you keep the house, but the plan still has to account for what that house is worth.

Key points

  • Chapter 13 plans must pay unsecured creditors at least what a Chapter 7 liquidation would have produced, and home value feeds that test.
  • A wholly unsecured second mortgage or HELOC may be treated as unsecured debt, but only if value supports it.
  • Non-residence property such as rentals may be subject to cramdown based on value.
  • Your attorney chooses the effective date; the report states it clearly.
  • Sales and refinances during a plan often benefit from a current, independent value.
  • This page is general information from an appraiser, not legal advice. Brian Ward is not a lawyer, and nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship; ask a bankruptcy attorney how the law applies to your case.

Keeping the house still means valuing it

Chapter 13 is a repayment case. Instead of a trustee selling property, you propose a plan that typically runs three to five years and pays creditors from your income. People often choose it to stop a foreclosure, catch up on missed mortgage payments, or protect equity that would be exposed in Chapter 7. Because you keep your property, it is easy to assume the value of the home does not matter. It does. The plan must satisfy several legal tests before the court will confirm it, and at least two of them can turn on what your real estate is worth. Your attorney prepares the plan and handles those tests; the appraisal supplies a defensible value so the numbers in the plan rest on evidence rather than estimates pulled from a listing site.

The best-interest-of-creditors test

One requirement for confirmation is often called the liquidation analysis or the best-interest-of-creditors test. In plain terms, unsecured creditors must receive at least as much through your plan as they would have received if your non-exempt property had been liquidated in a Chapter 7 case. To run that comparison, your attorney estimates what a Chapter 7 trustee would net from selling your home after liens, costs of sale, and your homestead exemption. If that hypothetical figure is large, the plan has to pay unsecured creditors at least that much over its term, which can raise your monthly payment. A higher home value means a higher minimum. An appraisal that is well supported, neither inflated nor shaded low, helps your attorney propose a plan the trustee can accept without a fight.

Valuing a second mortgage or HELOC

Chapter 13 offers a tool Chapter 7 does not: in the right facts, a junior lien on your home that is completely unsecured can be treated as unsecured debt. That happens when the value of the home is less than the balance owed on the first mortgage, leaving nothing at all for the second loan or line of credit to attach to. Proving it requires a court finding about value, usually through a motion that your attorney files. The appraisal is the core evidence. Even a small cushion of value above the first mortgage can defeat the motion, so this is a situation where precision and documentation matter most. Our guide on lien stripping covers the details. Brian writes these reports knowing the lender may hire its own appraiser to look for weaknesses.

Rentals, cars, and cramdown

Your principal residence gets special protection from changes to the primary mortgage. Other real estate does not. If you own a rental house, a duplex, or a vacant lot, Chapter 13 may allow the secured claim on that property to be limited to the property's value, with the remainder of the loan treated as unsecured. Lawyers call this a cramdown. Whether it is available, and on what terms, is a legal question for your attorney. What the court will need is a credible value for the property, often measured close to the filing or confirmation date. For investment property, Brian looks at income features too, such as unit count, rents in the area, and condition of each unit. The report type and the effective date should be agreed with your attorney before work begins.

Which effective date applies

Chapter 13 cases sometimes involve more than one relevant date. Exemptions are generally fixed as of the petition date. Some valuation questions in a plan may look to the confirmation date or the plan's effective date instead, and courts have not always treated every issue the same way. That is a legal call, and Brian does not make it. What he does is write the report to the date your attorney specifies, state that date plainly, and choose comparable sales that reflect the market at that point in time. If the case later needs a value at a different date, for example when a plan is modified or a property is sold during the case, an updated or second report can be prepared and quoted separately.

Selling or refinancing during the plan

Life does not pause for five years. During a Chapter 13 plan, some homeowners decide to sell, refinance, or pay off the plan early, and those moves usually require court approval obtained through the attorney and trustee. A current appraisal can show that a proposed sale price is reasonable or that a refinance makes sense. A Pre-Listing style valuation can also help you set an asking price that the trustee will view as fair to creditors.
