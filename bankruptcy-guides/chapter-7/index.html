Your Home and a Chapter 7 Filing: Where the Appraisal Fits

In Chapter 7 the trustee asks one practical question about your house, and a credible appraisal is how you answer it.

Key points

  • The trustee subtracts liens, costs of sale, and your exemption from market value to see whether a sale would help creditors.
  • Value is generally measured as of the petition date, which may call for a retrospective report.
  • Interior photos and condition notes give the trustee evidence an automated estimate cannot.
  • Desktop reports fit clear, low-dispute cases; inspected reports fit close calls and condition-heavy homes.
  • Bring the report to your attorney before the 341 meeting so the value is ready to discuss.
  • This page is general information from an appraiser, not legal advice. Brian Ward is not a lawyer, and nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship; ask a bankruptcy attorney how the law applies to your case.

What the trustee is actually looking for

A Chapter 7 trustee is appointed to gather property that can be sold for the benefit of unsecured creditors. With a house, the trustee works through simple arithmetic: start with what the property would likely sell for, subtract the mortgage and any other liens, subtract realistic costs of selling, and then subtract the homestead exemption you claim. If little or nothing is left, a sale would not help creditors and the trustee usually has no reason to pursue it. If a meaningful amount remains, the trustee may try to sell the home or negotiate with you to buy back that equity. The first number in that chain is market value, and it drives everything after it. When the value on your schedules is supported by a written appraisal rather than a guess or a website estimate, the conversation with the trustee starts on firmer ground.

Why the petition date matters

Bankruptcy law generally looks at your property as it stood on the day the case was filed. For that reason, your attorney might want the value pinned to a particular earlier date instead of the date of the appraiser's visit. If the petition was filed a few weeks or months ago, Brian writes a retrospective report: he inspects or researches the property now, then selects comparable sales and market evidence that reflect conditions on the filing date. If you have not filed yet, the appraisal can be timed close to the planned filing so the value on Schedule A/B is current. In both cases the report spells out the effective date, so the trustee knows precisely what day the value applies to. Your attorney decides which date to use; Brian makes sure the report matches it.

Condition counts, and it has to be documented

Many homes in a Chapter 7 case have been stretched thin along with the household budget. A roof past its service life, a failed water heater, cracked tile, an unpermitted garage conversion, or a yard that has gone untended can all lower what a buyer would pay. An automated estimate cannot see any of that. On an inspected assignment, Brian photographs the interior and exterior, notes deferred maintenance and damage, and explains in the report how the condition affects the comparison to recently sold homes. The trustee does not have to take anyone's word for it, because the photos and the reasoning are in the file. Honest documentation works in both directions: if the home has been updated, the report says so too. The goal is a value a neutral reader would find credible.

Desktop or inspected report?

Not every Chapter 7 case needs someone walking through the house. If the home is fairly ordinary, the equity is plain to see, and no one anticipates a challenge, your attorney can get a supported schedules figure without an on-site visit from a $449 Desktop report or a $299 Basic Desktop. If condition is the central question, if equity sits near the exemption limit, or if the trustee has already pushed back on the value, the inspected $699 Standard report carries more weight, since photos of the interior make the case on their own. A Drive-By at $649 sits between the two when only the exterior and street setting need to be confirmed. Your attorney usually knows how closely the trustee will look, so Brian often confirms the report type with the law office before scheduling.

At and after the 341 meeting

About a month after filing, you attend the meeting of creditors, often called the 341 meeting, which is frequently held by video. The trustee asks questions under oath, and the value of your home is a common topic. Having the appraisal already provided through your attorney means you can point to a professional report instead of explaining how you arrived at a figure. Sometimes the trustee accepts the value and moves on. Sometimes the trustee orders a broker opinion or another appraisal and compares the two. If the numbers differ, the details in the report, such as the comparable sales chosen, the condition photos, and the adjustments, are what your attorney uses to discuss it.
